The Pages Ranking Fine But Losing the Click

A validated CTR-opportunity ranking model for content portfolios — where a rule-based baseline scores below random at the top of the queue, and why the fix wasn't a bigger model.

Lane: CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring Data: March 2026 warehouse snapshot 91,974 page-months · 55 clients 8-week internship, 7 audited notebooks
Median click-through rate by position tier, visible pages, March 2026 (n = 9,733 / 44,463 / 17,631 / 17,303 / 2,844). The whole model rests on this gradient being real — it is.
Abstract
  1. Among pages that already rank somewhere reasonable, which ones should an editor review first because their click-through rate underperforms for their position — and does that priority order actually matter?
  2. On a March-2026 snapshot of FlyRank's search warehouse (91,974 visible page-months, 55 clients), I built a position-tier-adjusted CTR-gap label, validated a rule-based baseline against a logistic-regression model on a client-grouped holdout split, and audited both for leakage and overclaiming.
  3. The volume-weighted baseline scores below random at the top of the queue (precision@50 = 0.16 against an 18.8% base rate), while the validated model reaches 0.48 — better than 2.5× — on clients neither model was trained on.
  4. A companion audit found the warehouse's content-freshness field cannot yet support refresh-timing claims (82% of candidate pages show "updates" dated after the analysis window) — reported directly rather than smoothed over.
  5. The result ships as a human-reviewed, decision-support action playbook — 44,560 ranked pages, reason codes, and monitoring triggers — not an automated system, and not a causal claim about what fixes clicks.
01 · Introduction

A queue is only useful if the order is right

Content teams can't review every page every week. Some pages already rank fine but still under-capture clicks relative to peers at the same position — the question this study asks is whether that gap is real, measurable, and strong enough to prioritize a limited editorial review budget on.

This is a self-directed capstone from FlyRank's ML internship (Applied Search Intelligence track). The program offers four predefined lanes plus a freestyle option, locked by the end of Week 4; every weekly notebook from Week 1 onward built toward this paper.

Why this lane, over the other three

Four lanes were on the table: Ranking Signal Analysis (which safe signals associate with visibility), Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring (which pages are growing, declining, or worth review), Structured Content Archetype Clustering (grouping content into performance archetypes), and CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring — the one chosen here.

The case for it came from the starter data itself, not a preference: 55.8% of pages in an early sample cleared a basic visibility bar (real impressions, a real position), and within that visible pool, 58.3% showed a measurable, position-tier-adjusted CTR gap versus their peers. Refresh Scoring was the closest runner-up — its declining_with_demand bucket was similarly large — but it overlapped heavily with the CTR-gap pages while giving a vaguer "trending down" target. A tier-adjusted CTR gap gave a sharper, more defensible definition of "underperforming" to start from, with room to fold decline/refresh signals in later as a secondary reason code (which the final playbook does, in a limited way — see Section 8).

Framed as a ranking problem, not a classification problem

The underlying decision — "which pages should an editor open first?" — is a "which ones first" pattern, which points to ranking/scoring rather than classification (there's no clean pass/fail state for a page, only a more-or-less continuous underperformance gap) or clustering (the goal was one ordered priority list, not undiscovered groups). That framing decision, made in Week 2 before any data was touched, shaped every choice downstream: the evaluation metric became precision@K rather than plain accuracy, and the label became a continuous gap rather than a hand-labeled category.

An early warning sign Before any model existed, a flat rule — rank pages by raw CTR alone, ignoring position — was tested against a tier-adjusted "true" priority ranking on the starter data. It scored 0% precision@50. Not a close miss: an almost completely different set of pages. That single number was the first piece of evidence that position-tier adjustment isn't a nice-to-have refinement, it's the whole mechanism.

The decision, the action, the cost of getting it wrong

Decision: which visible pages a content editor opens first in a weekly review cycle. Who acts: a content reviewer with a fixed weekly capacity, working down a ranked queue. Action: review title and meta description (or, in a small, provisionally-flagged minority of cases, a fuller content refresh — see Limitations). Cost of a wrong call: the two mistakes are not symmetric. A false positive — a page flagged with nothing really wrong — costs an editor roughly 10-15 minutes of wasted review. A false negative — a genuinely underperforming, high-traffic page that never surfaces — is worse: it keeps quietly losing clicks until something bigger forces attention elsewhere. The queue is deliberately built to lean toward not missing high-impression pages, even at the cost of a few extra false positives, and that trade-off is stated here rather than folded silently into a metric.

02 · Data

One warehouse release, one mid-panel month

Source: the FlyRank ML Internship Warehouse, a gated Hugging Face release covering GSC (Google Search Console) and GA4 metrics aggregated per content page, per day, across many client portfolios. Two tables were used: fact_content_daily_performance (the month=2026-03 partition only) and dim_content (content-level metadata: type, intent, word count, search volume, creation/update dates). March 2026 was chosen deliberately as a mid-panel month — the release's own documentation flags the final month as a sealed test partition (a natural outcome window for any past→future label), and it was never touched while building label logic; a small _sample table drawn from that final month was used only once, early on, to test query mechanics.

Excluded, and why

  • GA4 / session / AI-referral columns — partial coverage for clients without GA4 access, and unrelated to a GSC-CTR lane.
  • Raw daily avg_position — replaced with the page-month reconstruction described above.
  • The sealed final-month table — reserved as an untouched holdout; used to test query mechanics only, never to build label logic.
  • content_updated_date as a trusted point-in-time field — investigated for a freshness signal (Section 7), found unusable for this purpose, and excluded from anything but the honestly-reported limitation itself (Section 7).

Data quality, as found, not as assumed

Two structural fields carried more missingness than expected once actually inspected. word_count was absent for a recurring share of rows across every weekly review pulled during this study — including 4 of the 10 highest-priority rows in the very first baseline queue. search_volume showed exact 0.0 values sitting next to pages with tens of thousands of real impressions, which is either a genuine zero-demand keyword or a missing/placeholder code — the paper does not resolve which, and the playbook (Section 8) routes such rows to manual review rather than assuming either.

Public-safe note: every client and content identifier in this warehouse is an opaque hash generated by the release itself. No domain names, URLs, raw exports, or client-identifying information appear anywhere in this paper or its accompanying notebooks.

03 · Methodology

Label, features, baseline, split, audit

The label

y = 1 if a page's CTR gap — its CTR minus the median CTR of other visible pages in the same position tier, that month — sits in the worst 10% of the distribution. This is an observed quantity computed directly from the March data, not a rule someone wrote by hand, and deliberately different from what the baseline rule itself optimizes (see below) — otherwise the baseline would be graded against the exact thing it was built to maximize, which is not a fair test.

Two signal checks, before building anything

SignalBehindVerdictEvidence
CTR vs. position tierCTR-fix flag logicCONFIRMED0.220% → 0.000% monotonically across 5 tiers, n = 2,844–44,463
Search volume vs. gap sizeQuick-win flag logicOPPOSITEmean gap 0.130 (zero-volume) → 0.013 (1000+ volume)

The first result licenses the whole approach: if CTR didn't actually vary by position tier, comparing a page against its tier peers would be meaningless. The second is a useful negative, not a failure — the naive "target high-search-volume keywords first" assumption behind classic quick-win logic runs backwards in this data: the biggest CTR gaps cluster among low-volume pages, not high-volume ones. That single check changed the design — the final score weights by observed impressions (real traffic already happening), never by theoretical search_volume, and search_volume was kept only as a minor structural feature rather than a scoring weight.

Features (6, all knowable at decision time)

position_tier, word_count, content_type, main_intent, search_volume, impressions — never CTR or the gap itself. Each was chosen because it describes something true about the page before this month's click data existed: where it currently ranks, what it is, how long it is, what a reader wants from it, and how much raw traffic it's already getting. None of them are constructed from the label.

Baseline (Week 4)

score = (tier-median CTR − page CTR) × impressions, for underperforming pages only. One reason code (ctr_below_tier_peers), one action (review_title_meta) — deliberately simple, with no fitted weights, so that it represents a real, defensible thing for a learned model to have to beat, rather than a strawman.

Split design

Grouped by client_hash_id, not random row-level. Pages from the same client tend to share templates, CMS quirks, and content niches — a random split lets the model partially learn to recognize a specific client rather than a generalizable relationship between structural signals and CTR underperformance, and that memorization would silently inflate every reported score. The grouped split produced 85,429 train rows across 31 clients and 6,545 test rows across 11 clients, with zero client overlap — every number in the Results section below comes from scoring clients the model never saw during training.

Leakage audit

The single most important check performed on this pipeline: deliberately adding ctr_gap itself as a feature — the exact quantity the label is thresholded from — and watching what happens. ROC-AUC jumped from 0.798 to a suspicious 1.0. A near-perfect score is a leak alert, not a win; the feature was removed, and 0.798 is the number reported everywhere else in this paper. 04 · Practicing the audit on someone else's work first

Two questions I'd ask of FlyRank's own published research

Before turning the same scrutiny on this study's own model, the same lens was applied — respectfully and concretely — to FlyRank's own public research report on the broader content portfolio. The goal was never to grade the paper; the industry standard it holds itself to is a genuinely useful one. The goal was to practice asking the kind of methodology question a careful reader should ask of any applied ML claim, including the ones in this paper.

Finding: "What Predicts Health?" (Random Forest feature importance for a composite Health Score)
Methodology question

The paper's own metrics section defines Health Score as impressions (30 pts) + position (30 pts) + CTR (20 pts) + scroll depth (20 pts). The model's top four ranked features are exactly those four ingredients. That's the textbook label-derived-feature pattern — the label is computed from these columns, and those same columns are the inputs. The paper is upfront that "importance is descriptive rather than causal," which is the right instinct, but the natural follow-up is: what does feature importance look like with those four definitional inputs removed, using only independent structural signals? Without that version, it's hard to know whether the model learned something real or simply rediscovered its own label's arithmetic.

Finding: "What Predicts Growth?" (Logistic Regression, reported as "71% holdout accuracy")
Methodology question

The paper's own portfolio-level split elsewhere shows roughly 62% growing pages vs. 38% declining. If the machine-learning sample used for this classifier carries a similar imbalance, a naive "always predict growing" rule would already score close to 62% — making "71% accuracy" closer to a single-digit improvement over the base rate than the headline number implies on its own. The paper reports the train/test split percentage but not the class balance of that split, so a reader can't tell how much of the 71% is genuine skill. A single added line — the base rate inside that specific holdout — would resolve it.

Both questions follow the same rule this paper tries to hold itself to everywhere else: a metric is only interpretable next to its base rate, and a feature set is only trustworthy once you've checked it doesn't already contain the label. Section 6 below applies exactly this discipline to this study's own model — a random-split "before" number, a grouped-split "after" number, and a deliberate leak added and then removed — rather than only pointing the lens outward.

05 · Results

The baseline loses where it matters, wins where it doesn't

Reading the baseline's own top 10 first

Before comparing anything to a model, the rule-based baseline's own ranked output was read row by row, the way an editor would. A few patterns stood out immediately: two of the top-ranked pages showed a click-through rate near zero (0.07%-0.12%) despite six-figure impression counts at strong positions — a pattern at least as consistent with a tracking or attribution anomaly as with a genuine title problem, and worth a manual check before any copy gets rewritten. A single client supplied three of the top ten rows, all sharing that same near-zero-CTR signature, suggesting one client-level issue rather than three independent content problems. And the single weakest pick in that top ten had the smallest actual gap in the list — it only ranked highly because a huge impression count inflated the score, foreshadowing exactly the problem the model comparison below would confirm at scale.

Both halves of this table are real, and both matter for different reasons. Across the whole score range, the baseline still tracks the true gap reasonably well (hence its high AUC) — the distortion is specifically at the very top, which is exactly where a fixed-size weekly queue lives. For the actual decision this model supports, precision@K is the right metric, and the learned model wins it clearly. Random Forest, tested as a stronger option, didn't earn its added complexity — it's within a few points of logistic regression on every metric, so logistic regression is the model kept forward.

The false alarms cluster at very low impression counts with genuinely strong (positive) CTR gaps: the model appears to read "statistically unusual relative to typical volume" as risk without reliably telling direction apart when there's very little traffic to go on — and two of three such cases were also missing word_count, echoing the same data-completeness issue named in Section 2.

Retraining the identical model under a naive random row-level split — rather than the grouped split used everywhere else in this paper — nearly doubles precision@50 (0.48 → 0.84) and pushes AUC to 0.913. Thirty-seven clients ended up on both sides of that split. None of that gain is real skill — it's the model partly memorizing client-specific patterns, the same failure mode named in Section 4's audit of the "71% accuracy" claim. The grouped numbers (0.48 / 0.798) are the honest ones, and the only ones this paper's other claims are built on.

06 · What was tried and rejected

Decisions that didn't make the final pipeline

A paper reads cleaner than the process that produced it. Four choices were seriously tried and then deliberately reversed — worth naming, since a rejected path is often as informative as the one that shipped.

✕
Random Forest as the production model
Tested against Logistic Regression on the identical grouped split. Precision@50 0.460 vs 0.480, precision@100 0.550 vs 0.520, AUC 0.790 vs 0.799 — essentially tied, no consistent winner. Kept Logistic Regression: same performance, and its coefficients are directly readable by a human reviewer.
✕
Weighting the score by search_volume ("quick-win" style)
The natural first instinct for a priority score is to favor high-demand keywords. The Section 3 signal check showed the opposite pattern in this data — mean CTR gap shrinks as search volume rises. Scoring by volume would have systematically pointed the queue at the wrong pages.
✕
A random row-level train/test split
The default, easier choice. Section 5's before/after comparison shows exactly why it was rejected: it inflates every metric by letting client identity leak across the split, and none of that inflation is real generalization.
✕
Trusting content_updated_date as a refresh-timing signal
Explored as a natural companion insight (older, stale, underperforming pages as a distinct archetype). Found unusable once inspected: 82% of candidate rows show an "update" dated after the March analysis window, meaning the field is a live snapshot, not point-in-time. The resulting archetype is flagged provisional and excluded from any actioned recommendation (Section 8).
07 · Limitations & honest framing

What this data cannot support yet

Named limitation The warehouse's content-freshness field (content_updated_date) is a live snapshot, not a point-in-time record — 82.0% of candidate rows show an "update" dated after the March 2026 window this study measures. Refresh-timing claims are explicitly not supported by this data as joined. The stale_underperformer archetype in Section 8 is flagged provisional for exactly this reason.
  • Single month. Every number here is March 2026. Whether the pattern holds in other months or seasons is untested.
  • Partial client coverage. Only 52.9% of clients (55 of 104) have any usable row this month; nothing here generalizes to clients without active GSC access.
  • Weak structural signal. Outside position and traffic, the remaining features (word count, content type, intent, search volume) each carry permutation importance under 0.01 — most of the model's skill is coming from where a page ranks and how much traffic it gets, not what the page is about.
  • Borderline errors near the threshold. Section 5's false-negative examples sit close to the label's own decision boundary; a small number of the highest-value pages are still missed, and that's worth monitoring rather than treating the 0.480 figure as a ceiling on real-world performance.
  • Observed, not causal. A CTR gap is a measured pattern relative to peers, not proof that a title rewrite will recover clicks, and not a claim about any Google ranking factor. That would need an actual before/after intervention, which this study doesn't run.

What would raise confidence in this model

Three concrete next steps, none of which this paper claims to have done: re-validating the same pipeline on a second, later mid-panel month to check the pattern isn't specific to March; running an actual A/B test on a batch of flagged pages to test whether the recommended action changes CTR, rather than only ranking existing gaps; and re-deriving a genuine point-in-time freshness signal (a monthly-partitioned content snapshot, rather than a single live table) to finally test the refresh-timing hypothesis this data currently can't support.

What a typical flagged page looks like

A representative big_miss_high_traffic page sits at top_3 or page_1, with a CTR near zero against a tier median above 20%, and impressions in the tens of thousands — real, valuable traffic that's mostly not converting to clicks. A representative quiet_underperformer shows the same directional gap at a smaller scale — thousands rather than tens of thousands of impressions — which is why it queues behind the high-traffic cases rather than ahead of them.

Human review, required before any action

  • Check tracking integrity before assuming a copy problem — pages with near-zero CTR at a strong position and large impressions are as likely to be a tracking/attribution anomaly as a genuine title issue (Section 5).
  • Flag rows missing word_count or search_volume for manual review rather than trusting the row at face value.
  • Cap any single client at roughly 10% of a weekly review batch — repeated client concentration was observed at the top of every ranked list produced during this study, both the baseline's and the model's.

What should not be automated

  • No auto-publishing of rewritten titles or meta descriptions — a person writes and approves the copy.
  • No acting on the stale_underperformer label until the content-freshness data problem is fixed with a real point-in-time source.
  • No treating the model's score as a forecast of recovered clicks — it is a priority ranking, not a guarantee.
  • No extending this logic to another lane, month, or client population without re-running the validation in Section 5 on that new slice first.

A first-week rollout, as a suggestion

  1. Pull the top 50 by score
    From the exported queue, restricted to clients with fewer than 5 pages already in the batch (the concentration cap).
  2. Triage before writing
    Separately flag any row with near-zero CTR at a strong position for a tracking check, and any row missing word_count/search_volume for a data check, before assigning copy work.
  3. Route the rest to editors
    By reason_code, so an editor sees why a page was flagged, not just that it was.
  4. Measure at 30 days
    CTR and impressions on the reviewed batch versus a comparable untouched sample — the only way to move any claim here from directional to causal.
